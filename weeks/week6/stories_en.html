Blaise Pascal

The Gambler's Paradox & Expected Value

"The heart has its reasons of which reason knows nothing."

In 1654, Chevalier de Méré presented Blaise Pascal with a famous gambling dilemma: How do you fairly divide the stakes of an unfinished dice game? Pascal corresponded with Pierre de Fermat, birthing modern probability theory and expected value. Pascal's Wager on God's existence became the first formal decision analysis under extreme uncertainty — laying the cornerstone for stochastic modeling!

🎲

Stanislaw Ulam

The Solitaire Genius

"Just play it through 100 times."

While lying in bed, Ulam invented the Monte Carlo method. He realized that complex mathematical problems can often be solved more easily experimentally through simulation than through pure formulas. Later, he used this technique to help calculate the hydrogen bomb. A casino visit by his uncle gave the whole thing its name.

🔥

Ludwig Boltzmann

Father of Statistical Mechanics

"Entropy is a measure of the craving for chaos." (S = k · log W)

In the late 19th century, Boltzmann defied classical physics by demonstrating that heat and pressure are not continuous fluids, but the statistical aggregate of trillions of randomly colliding atoms. His Boltzmann distribution (P ∝ e-E/kBT) and entropy formula S = k log W form the core foundation of modern stochastic simulation — from Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) to sampling generative AI models!

Andrei Markov

The Poet of Probability

Markov wasn't interested in physics, but in Alexander Pushkin. He analyzed 20,000 letters from "Eugene Onegin" to show that sequences of letters form a chain. Today, his Markov chains power everything – from Google searches to financial forecasts. Chaos has a system.

The Black Swan

The Unpredictable

Nassim Taleb warned us of 'Black Swans': events so rare that no statistics predict them, but that change the world. Monte Carlo simulations help us at least play through these extreme scenarios. In Week 6, you'll learn how to prepare for the impossible.

JAX-PRNG Keys

Cryptographic Randomness

In JAX, randomness is a pure function. You hold the key. If you forget to 'split' it, time stands still – every result becomes identical. It's like Groundhog Day for programmers. Use Threefry encryption to unleash millions of paths simultaneously on the GPU.
