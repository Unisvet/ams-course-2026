The "Italian Navigator"

Enrico Fermi was a master of "back-of-the-envelope" calculations. During the Trinity test, the first nuclear explosion, he dropped scraps of paper as the shockwave reached him. By observing how far they traveled, he estimated the bomb's yield at about 10 kilotons of TNT—before the sensors were even analyzed. He was remarkably close to the actual value of 21 kilotons.

"In simulation as in reality: A clever model in the mind is often more powerful than a thousand measurements."

The Great Explainer

Richard Feynman hated unnecessary complexity. During the investigation into the Challenger disaster, he demonstrated on live television with a simple glass of ice water how rocket O-rings lose their elasticity in the cold. He turned a highly complex engineering problem into a tangible experiment.

"If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it. This applies to your code as well."

The Father of AI

Alan Turing was thinking about "thinking machines" when computers still consisted of gears and vacuum tubes. At Bletchley Park, he cracked the Enigma codes not just through pure mathematics, but by building a machine (the "Bombe") that simulated another machine. He understood that we need machines to master the complexity of the world.

"Only a machine can decipher the mind of another machine."
