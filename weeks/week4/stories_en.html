The Challenger Disaster

When the space shuttle Challenger exploded in 1986, Richard Feynman was part of the investigative commission. While others got lost in mountains of documents, Feynman demonstrated the cause—the failure of the O-rings in cold weather—simply with a glass of ice water in front of rolling cameras. His message was clear: Complex simulations are worthless if we do not intuitively understand the underlying physics. JAX allows us today to pour this physical intuition directly into differentiable code.

Room at the Bottom

In 1959, Feynman gave his legendary talk on nanotechnology. He predicted that we would one day manipulate information at the atomic level. 'Silicon Ascension' is the modern implementation of this vision: We use JAX and GPUs to simulate millions of particles simultaneously, as if we were the architects on the quantum level ourselves.

Quantum Simulation

Feynman was the first to recognize: If you want to simulate nature, you have to build computers that function like nature—that is, quantum-mechanically and massively parallel. His 1981 talk laid the foundation for what we do today: With JAX and TPUs, we map the parallel structure of reality.
